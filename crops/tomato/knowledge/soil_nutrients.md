# Tomato Soil pH and Nutrients

**Crop**: Tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*)
**Topic**: Soil and nutrition

## Soil pH
- Extension guidance places the target soil pH for tomato at roughly 6.2 to 6.8. Georgia Extension recommends 6.0 to 6.8, and North Carolina's plasticulture guidance treats pH 6.5 as sufficient for calcium and magnesium availability.
- Test the soil before liming or fertilising. A soil test is the only reliable way to know whether pH or calcium is actually limiting.
- If pH is already in range, adding more lime as a calcium source is not helpful and can push pH above the target range. Gypsum supplies calcium without raising pH.
- In this project the soil pH probe is uncalibrated, so readings show relative change for a plant over time, not absolute soil pH. Confirm any pH problem with a soil test.

## Calcium and blossom-end rot
- Blossom-end rot (a dark, sunken patch at the flower end of the fruit) is a calcium problem in the fruit, not an infectious disease.
- It can come from too little calcium in the soil (often linked to low pH) or from poor calcium transport inside the plant.
- Large swings in soil moisture increase blossom-end rot; keep watering steady, especially during fruit set.
- Applying too much fertiliser at once, and excessive nitrogen, can reduce calcium uptake and raise blossom-end rot.
- Foliar calcium sprays are only a short-term fix and often work poorly; fix soil calcium and watering instead.

## Nutrient stress and disease
- Nutrient stress (for example nitrogen or potassium shortage) and drought stress can make plants more susceptible to early blight.
- Excess nitrogen is discouraged for target spot management.
- Yellowing of lower leaves can come from nutrient shortage or water stress rather than infection. Check for lesions, spots or mould before treating it as disease.
